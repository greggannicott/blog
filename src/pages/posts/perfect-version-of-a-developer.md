I was recently listening to the [Roles & Goals](https://relay.fm/focused/117) episode of the podcast Focused.

In it, they discuss the idea of specifying roles (or identities in [Atomic Habits](https://jamesclear.com/atomic-habits) parlance) that you either already fit, or strive to be. By deciding these, you essentially define something that you can compare yourself against. They act as both goals and as a guide for when it comes to making decisions.

You don't need to be limited by a single role, and they don't need to be strictly professional. For example, the roles I chose when doing this were:

- Consultant
- Culture Vulture (I couldn't find a better title, and I _really_ did try!)
- Developer
- Father
- Gardener
- Handy Man
- Healthy Man
- Husband
- Productive Man
- Reader
- Runner
- Son
- Student
- Well Presented Man
- Writer

It's safe to say I can't consider myself to be all of them. Some are aspirational (I'd love to do more in the garden, or more specifically own something that resembles one) and some are ones that I would love to improve upon (although I read, I'd love to read more books).

Something I found particularly useful from the episode was the suggestion that not only do you define the roles, but you write out what you consider to be the perfect version of that role. This gives you an opportunity to asses how you're doing and also to define something to strive for.

This was not only a fun exercise but also quite enlightening.

Before a provide an example of a role I defined, I want to share couple tips for this stage of the process:

- Write out some high level categories for what makes up the role you're writing about. For example, if you're defining the perfect developer, the categories could include Problem Solving, Mentoring, and Communication. This not only provides a way to group them and make them easier to read, but more importantly it gives you different aspects to focus on. What does make a developer good at communication?
- Asses others. Look at other people who perform the role you are writing about, and note down not only what do you think they do well at in that role, but also what do they do not so well? Which aspects are you jealous of and which would you like to avoid? This stage is particularly good fun and is surprisingly therapeutic.

# The Perfect Developer

So, given all of that, below is _my_ idea of a perfect developer. I can assure you that I don't tick all the boxes, but it acts as a great list to check occasionally to see how I'm doing and to also use as a guide on what to focus on in terms of self improvement.

_The perfect developer is someone who..._

## General

- cares
- writes their own tools to help themselves
- understands all stories in the epic
- is open to the idea that their approach is wrong
- enjoys their job
- looks to optimise business workflow
- is quick on the keyboard
- is proficient with the software they use
- is understanding of mistakes
- prefers pragmatic approach over dogmatic approach
- is always available and approachable for help
- is open to alternative approaches
- is happy to admit when they are wrong
- challenges the accepted state of things
- offers to help without you having to seek it
- is considered and is not rash
- does not move on in a conversation until they fully understand what is being explained
- is not afraid to say what the other person does not want to hear

## Problem Solving

- enjoys puzzle solving
- can take a methodical approach to solving problems
- simplifies the situation
- narrows down where the failure is using appropriate techniques
- swaps components out do understand the cause
- attempts to understand the code
- uses the best tools available
- writes tests to recreate the scenario

## Estimation

- pushes back when estimates are challenged
- doesn't try to impress with estimates
- doesn't downplay work involved with estimates
- takes into account unknowns and risks when estimating
- obtains more time to provide an estimate if required
- understands what is being estimated (the level of which depends on how early on something is being estimated)

## Coding

- writes easy to understand code
- writes unit tests when logic is involved (eg. put x in, expect y out)
- doesn't push for 100% coverage
- tries to break their own code
- leaves code tidier than they found it
- uses comments sparingly
- doesn't over engineer a solution
- is prolific
- keeps up with new language/framework features

## Quality

- enjoys trying to break things
- gets a thrill from highlighting issues during meetings
- has a full understanding of the story before starting
- cares about the details
- raises concerns about how the app functions
- considers the user
- tries to find faults in the ACs
- proactively raises tickets

## Communication

- can communicate well verbally
- can communicate well via the written word
- uses formatting and well structured paragraphs etc to make things easier to read/follow
- keeps others informed of changes that might impact them
- updates requirements when they change
- logs the reasons behind the requirement changes when they happen
- keeps tickets up to date (eg. ticket state reflects reality)
- injects personality into everything they write and say
- enjoys explaining things to others
- can reassure others that the task is in hand
- shares what they learn
- shares the tools they find useful
- writes a bug that anyone can recreate
- writes ACs that are not ambiguous
- can break complex subjects down into something that is easy to understand
- has engaging presentations
- shares what they learn
- shares the tools they find useful

## Meetings

- is focused on the meeting
- leads
- can chair a meeting
- makes sure everyone understands what _is_ being agreed upon in a meeting
- makes sure everyone understands what _was_ agreed upon following the meeting
- keeps a meeting focussed
- updates requirements when they change
- is open to compromise
- is open to being wrong

## Code Reviews

- teaches through code reviews
- provides positive comments in code reviews
- explains their reasoning in code reviews
- is prepared to compromise
- enjoys trying to break things and find faults
- can spot bugs by reading the code
- is familiar with team standards
- is aware of new language features

## Mentoring

- teaches through code reviews
- enjoys explaining things to others
- enjoys trying to help others
- documents what is being taught for future reference
- always makes themselves available to help, no matter how busy they are

## Self improvement

- looks for ways to better themselves
- exposes themselves to new things via articles, videos etc
- looks for ways to improve their workflow
- reads the manual
- enjoys trying new things
- wants to go down an abstraction layer to understand how something works
- contributes to open source software
- releases hobby software
- puts themselves out of their comfort zones

## Dependability

- volunteers when help is requested in meetings
- is not afraid to take on problems they're unsure of in areas they're unsure of
- considers deadlines
